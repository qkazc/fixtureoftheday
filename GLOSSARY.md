# Glossary

Shipping terms used in the game, in plain English. Kept in alphabetical order.

<!-- Add entries as: **Term**: one or two plain sentences. -->

**Address commission**: A percentage of the freight that the shipowner pays
back to the charterer. It's essentially a discount dressed up as a commission
and is often around 2.5–3.75%.

**Ballast leg**: The part of a voyage where the ship sails empty, usually from
where it finished its last job to the port where it will load the next cargo.
The owner pays for this leg but earns nothing on it.

**Bid**: The rate a charterer says they will pay. Charterers bid; owners
offer. A bid usually starts below where the charterer expects to end up.

**Brokerage**: The commission a shipbroker earns for arranging a deal, usually
paid by the shipowner as a percentage of the freight (often around 1.25% per
broker). In this game, this is your income.

**Bunkers**: Ship's fuel. The name dates from the coal bunkers that steamships
stored their fuel in. Bunker costs are usually the biggest single voyage
expense.

**Capesize**: The largest common dry bulk carriers, roughly 150,000–210,000
DWT, mostly carrying iron ore and coal. Too big for the Panama Canal (and
historically the old Suez Canal), so they sail around Cape Horn and the Cape
of Good Hope, hence the name.

**Charterer**: The party who hires a ship. In dry bulk this is usually the
cargo owner, or a trader shipping cargo on someone else's behalf.

**Chokepoint**: A narrow passage that many shipping routes must pass through,
such as the Strait of Malacca, Suez Canal or Panama Canal. A closure or queue
at one can add days or weeks to voyages.

**Counter-offer (counter)**: A reply that rejects the other side's number and
names a new one, normally closer than before. Rounds of counters narrow the
gap until both sides meet or one walks away.

**Deadweight (DWT)**: The total weight in tonnes a ship can carry: cargo plus
fuel, fresh water, stores and crew. Because fuel and stores take up some of
it, the cargo a ship can actually lift is always a bit less than its DWT.

**Deal zone**: The range of rates both sides could accept: above the owner's
minimum and below the charterer's maximum. A general negotiation term (also
called ZOPA, "zone of possible agreement"). If the two limits don't overlap,
no deal is possible.

**Demurrage**: Money the charterer pays the owner when loading or discharging
takes longer than the time allowed in the contract. It compensates the owner
for the ship being stuck in port.

**Discharge port**: The port where the cargo is unloaded at the end of the
laden leg.

**Draft (draught)**: How deep a ship sits in the water. A loaded ship sits
deeper, so a shallow port or channel can stop a ship from loading fully.

**Dry bulk**: Unpackaged cargo loaded loose into a ship's holds, such as iron
ore, coal, grain, bauxite or fertiliser.

**Enquiry**: A charterer's request to brokers to find a ship for a cargo:
what, how much, from where to where, and when (the laycan). Brokers get many a
day and must choose which to work on.

**Firm offer**: An offer the other side can accept straight away to close the
deal, usually with a short deadline. If it's declined, talks often end. Unlike
a counter, it says "this is my number".

**Fixture**: A concluded charter deal: owner and charterer have agreed the
ship, cargo, rate and terms. Brokers say a ship has been "fixed". It's where
this game gets its name.

**Freight**: The money a charterer pays the shipowner for carrying a cargo. In
dry bulk it is usually quoted in US dollars per tonne of cargo.

**Freight index**: A daily published rate for a standard route and ship size,
such as the Baltic Exchange's routes for Capesizes from Brazil to China.
Brokers read them each morning to see where the market is.

**Knot**: A speed of one nautical mile per hour. A ship doing 13 knots covers
13 × 24 = 312 nautical miles a day.

**Laden leg**: The part of a voyage where the ship is carrying cargo, from the
load port to the discharge port.

**Laycan**: Short for laydays/cancelling: the date window in which the ship
must arrive at the load port. Too early and the charterer needn't load it yet;
after the last day the charterer can cancel.

**Load port**: The port where the cargo is loaded onto the ship.

**Nautical mile (nm)**: The distance unit used at sea, equal to 1,852 metres
(about 1.15 ordinary miles).

**Offer**: The rate an owner asks for their ship. It usually starts above
where the owner expects to settle.

**Open date**: The day a ship becomes free for its next job. Together with the
open port it makes up the ship's open position.

**Open position**: Where and when a ship becomes free for its next job,
usually the port where it finishes discharging its current cargo. Brokers
circulate lists of open ships to charterers.

**Opex (operating expenses)**: What it costs every day just to own and run the
ship, whether or not it is working: crew wages, insurance, maintenance,
spares, stores. Opex is not a voyage cost.

**Panamax**: A dry bulk carrier of roughly 65,000–85,000 DWT, originally the
largest size that fit the old Panama Canal locks.

**Port costs (port disbursements)**: Fees paid at each port call: harbour
dues, pilots, tugs, berth fees, agency fees. On a voyage charter the shipowner
usually pays these.

**Position list**: A broker's list of ships with where and when each becomes
free (its open position). Brokers read it against their cargoes to spot which
ships fit which jobs.

**Rate idea**: The rate a charterer says it expects to pay. A guide for the
broker, not a hard ceiling: most charterers will go a little higher for the
right ship.

**Sea margin**: An allowance (often around 5%) added to calculated sea time to
cover bad weather and currents.

**Shipbroker**: An intermediary who matches ships with cargoes and negotiates
the deal between owner and charterer, earning a commission on the freight.

**Shipowner**: The company that owns (or controls) a ship and offers it for
hire.

**Supramax**: A mid-sized dry bulk carrier of roughly 50,000–60,000 DWT,
usually with its own cranes, so it can load and discharge at ports without
shore cranes. A workhorse for grain, coal, fertiliser and minerals.

**TCE (Time Charter Equivalent)**: A voyage's earnings converted into dollars
per day: (freight − voyage costs) ÷ voyage days. It lets an owner compare a
voyage-charter offer with a time-charter offer that pays a daily rate.

**Time charter**: A contract where the charterer hires the ship for a period
of time and pays a daily rate (called "hire"). The charterer pays for fuel and
port costs; the owner still pays opex.

**Voyage charter**: A contract to carry one specific cargo from A to B for a
freight rate per tonne. The owner pays fuel and port costs out of that
freight.

**Voyage costs**: Costs that exist only because of a particular voyage: mainly
bunkers, port costs and commissions. Different from opex, which the owner pays
regardless.

**Voyage estimate**: A quick calculation of a voyage's days, costs and TCE for
a given ship, cargo and rate. Brokers and owners run these constantly to judge
whether a deal makes sense.
